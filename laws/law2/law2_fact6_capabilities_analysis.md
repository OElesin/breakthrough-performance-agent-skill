# Fact 6: Capabilities Analysis
**Law:** Law 2 — Competitive position determines your options
**Position in Law:** Fact 3 of 3

---

## Purpose
Develop an externally validated, fact-based view of which capabilities truly create competitive advantage, where the gaps are, and which capabilities need to be built, strengthened, or sourced externally.

---

## Goal
The goal of capabilities analysis is to develop a fact-based assessment of your capabilities, as viewed by your customers, suppliers, and employees. It answers questions such as: which capabilities truly differentiate us from the competition in the eyes of these stakeholders? Where do we have the biggest gaps?

Chris Zook, in his book *Unstoppable*, shows how many companies have found "hidden assets" that allow them to develop new capabilities and thus redefine their core business.

Combined with an analysis of profit pools and changing customer needs, capabilities analysis allows you to determine:
- Which capabilities you need to invest in most aggressively to defend against harmful shifts in the profit pool
- Which capabilities can be leveraged to increase your share of your own profit pool
- How you can meet your customers' most important needs better than the competition
- Which capabilities might need to be outsourced

---

## Diagnostic Question
> "What are the few capabilities that are creating a competitive advantage for you? Which are missing and which ones need to be strengthened or acquired?"

---

## Data Required

- **Customer and supplier interviews** on key capabilities, strengths, and gaps
- **Competitor research** — analyst reports, industry surveys, interviews with ex-employees, suppliers, etc.
- **Employee feedback on best practices** — across businesses, across geographies, and historically

---

## Analytical Approach

### Four-step capabilities analysis process (Figure A3.12)

| Step | Description | Key Success Factor |
|------|-------------|---------------------|
| 1. Create a broad list of capabilities most relevant in your industry | Build the candidate list | Seek opinions of industry analysts, employees across functions, and a select number of key customers and suppliers |
| 2. Design and conduct surveys | Gather structured input | You'll need at least three different surveys — for customers, suppliers, and employees; decide which capabilities belong in which survey, since some questions aren't appropriate for certain groups |
| 3. Analyse data | Build the ratings chart | Important to develop this chart for major suppliers, by customer segment, and by employee level/function |
| 4. Draw implications | Decide what to act on | Not every gap needs to be addressed; combine with profit-pool and customer-needs analysis to set correct priorities |

### Reading the capabilities chart (Figure A3.11)
The chart rates capabilities — illustrative sample categories include Strategy, Products, Processes, Management Experience, and Decision Making, though every organisation must determine its own most relevant set — on a stacked bar of total responses split into:
- World Class
- Above Average
- Below Average

For each capability, identify what to exploit today versus what needs to be built, with a planned action attached (e.g. "increase salesforce experience levels").

---

## Interpretation Guide

**Healthy signal:**
- Capabilities rated "world class" align with where profit pools are growing and customer needs are shifting
- Internal (employee) and external (customer/supplier) ratings broadly agree

**Warning signal:**
- A capability critical to defending your profit pool is rated "below average"
- Internal and external ratings diverge sharply — e.g. employees rate a capability highly while customers see a clear gap, signalling a blind spot
- "Hidden assets" exist that aren't being leveraged into new capabilities or business redefinition

**Ambiguous result — probe further:**
- If ratings diverge across stakeholder groups, ask: whose view should carry more weight for this specific capability, and why?
- If a gap is identified, ask: does this need to be built internally, acquired, or outsourced?

## Agent Prompting Guidance

**Opening question:**
> "Last fact in Law 2 — capabilities. Setting aside internal assumptions, if you asked your customers and suppliers directly, which of your capabilities do you think they'd say truly sets you apart? And where might they say you fall short?"

**Follow-up probes if stuck:**
- "Which capabilities do you believe are genuinely world class for your business today?"
- "Have you had direct feedback from customers or suppliers on this, or is it mostly internal perception?"
- "Are there any 'hidden assets' — capabilities you have but aren't fully exploiting — that could open up a new part of the business?"
- "Is there a capability gap concerning enough that you'd consider outsourcing rather than building it internally?"

**Synthesising the finding:**
> "So [capability] looks like a genuine differentiator, while [capability] is a gap — and notably, internal and external perception of that gap [agree/differ]. Does that feel accurate, and does it change how you'd prioritise investment?"

**Signal to move on:**
User has identified which capabilities are genuine differentiators, which are gaps, and has a view on whether internal and external perceptions align. This completes Law 2 — signal readiness to move to Law 3.

**Law 2 closing synthesis prompt:**
> "We've now completed the three facts of Law 2. You have a view of your market position and whether you're earning what it should entitle you to (ROA/RMS), the size and growth dynamics of your market and where you're gaining or losing share (Market Map), and a fact-based, externally validated view of which capabilities differentiate you or fall short (Capabilities Analysis). Before we move to Law 3, what's your headline finding from Law 2 — what's the single most important thing you've learned about your ability to compete in this market?"
