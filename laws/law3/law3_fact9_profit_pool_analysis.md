# Fact 9: Profit-Pool Analysis
**Law:** Law 3 — Customers and profit pools don't stand still
**Position in Law:** Fact 3 of 3

---

## Purpose
Map where profit actually sits across your industry's entire value chain — not just where revenue is — revealing where to compete, what to acquire, which customers to pursue, and where profit is migrating toward or away from you.

---

## Goal
The objective is to map total profits earned in your industry at all points along the value chain. This answers the basic question about an industry: where and how are companies making money?

**Critical insight:** The pattern of profit concentration is often very different from the pattern of revenue concentration. A large revenue share does not guarantee a large profit share — and a business can be in a high-revenue segment while most of the industry's profit sits elsewhere in the value chain.

Mapping profit pools over time also answers questions about industry evolution:
- Why did profit pools form where they did?
- How have they changed over time?
- Are the forces that created or shifted the pools likely to change?

**What a general manager who understands profit pools can do:**
- Identify new sources of profits in low-margin industries
- Chart acquisition and expansion strategies
- Make good decisions about which customers to pursue and which channels to use
- Guide product, pricing, and operating decisions effectively

---

## Diagnostic Question
> "How much of the profit pool do you have today? How is it likely to change in the future? What are the opportunities and threats?"

---

## Data Required

- **List of channels, products, and sequential value-chain activities in the profit pool**
  - What it is: A complete map of where profit can potentially sit in your industry, from raw materials to end customer
  - Where to get it: Industry analysis, value-chain mapping, competitor research

- **Estimated total profit from all channels, all products, and all value-chain activities**
  - What it is: The aggregate profit pool size, covering all players
  - Where to get it: Public filings, industry databases, analyst reports, triangulated estimates

- **Point estimates of profit for your business and for competitors by channel, product, and value-chain activity**
  - What it is: How the total pool is divided at each point in the value chain
  - Where to get it: Internal management accounts plus competitor financial data by segment

---

## Analytical Approach

### Two chart formats — choose based on question

**Format 1 (Figure A3.21 — total profit dollars):** Mosaic chart comparing "Industry Operating Profits 3–5 Years Ago" vs "Industry Operating Profits This Year." X-axis shows Channels or Products or Activities; Y-axis shows Competitors stacked within each column. Your company is shaded distinctly.
- Best for: seeing where you and all key competitors participate, and how relative size has shifted
- Makes it easier to spot relative movement among competitors and trends that represent threats or opportunities

**Format 2 (Chapter 4 version — profit percentage):** X-axis shows sales dollars; Y-axis shows operating profit percentage.
- Best for: understanding where you are likely to get a good return on investment by pursuing a particular pool

*Alternative:* Show sales by segment on X-axis and percent operating margin on Y-axis.

**Point of arrival example (Figure A3.21):** Share of profit pool from 20% to 50%.

### Four-step process (Figure A3.22)

| Step | Description | Key Success Factor |
|------|-------------|---------------------|
| 1. Map your business's value chain | End-to-end from raw materials to finished product or delivery | Go beyond traditional definitions — look at your business from the perspective of your customers, suppliers, and competitors |
| 2. Estimate the size of your profit pool | Quantify the profit at each step | Initially focus on the biggest pieces — largest companies or those accounting for a large portion of industry profits; use operating profit (not net or gross) |
| 3. Reality-check your estimates | Triangulate top-down and bottom-up | Compare top-down total profit pool estimates with bottom-up estimates for each value-chain activity; collect additional data if needed |
| 4. Draw implications | Turn the map into strategic action | Use profit-pool map to draw strategic and tactical insights: new sources of profit, acquisition and expansion strategies, customer segments/channels to pursue, adjustments to product, pricing, and operating decisions |

## Interpretation Guide

**Healthy signal:**
- Your share of the profit pool is growing faster than your share of revenue — you are moving toward higher-profit parts of the value chain
- You participate in the segments with the highest and most durable profit concentration

**Warning signal:**
- You hold a large revenue share in a segment where the profit pool is thin or shrinking — you are working hard for little return
- A competitor is steadily increasing their share of the profit pool in segments you currently occupy — a leading indicator of future competitive pressure
- The forces that historically created profit concentration in your segment (e.g. information asymmetry, switching costs, regulatory protection) are eroding

**Ambiguous result — probe further:**
- If profit pool size is hard to estimate, ask: which one or two value-chain steps are most obviously profitable? Start there and work outward.
- If your share looks stable but competitors are entering, ask: are entrants absorbing new pool growth while you hold only legacy position?

## Additional Tools

**Diagnostic tools:** SWOT analysis (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats); competitor analysis; customer needs analysis (see Fact 7); supplier analysis; governmental and regulatory environment assessment; scenario planning; adjacencies mapping; value-chain mapping including power shifts among key players.

**Implementation tools:** war games and real-options analysis, as well as many of the tools mentioned across other sections of this appendix.

## Agent Prompting Guidance

**Opening question:**
> "The final fact in Law 3 is about profit pools — not just where your revenue sits, but where the actual profit in your industry is concentrated. Do you have a sense of which parts of your value chain are genuinely profitable versus which look good on revenue but thin on margin?"

**Follow-up probes if stuck:**
- "If you mapped your industry from raw input to end customer, which step do you think captures the most profit?"
- "Is your share of industry profit growing or shrinking relative to your revenue share?"
- "Are there parts of your value chain where a competitor or new entrant has quietly built a disproportionate profit position?"
- "Have profit pools in your industry shifted significantly in the last three to five years? What drove that?"

**Synthesising the finding:**
> "So the biggest concentration of profit in your industry sits at [value-chain point], and you currently capture roughly [X]% of the pool. The direction of travel looks like [growing/shrinking share], driven by [force]. Does that feel like an accurate picture — and does it suggest any part of the value chain you should be moving toward or defending?"

**Signal to move on:**
User has a working picture of where industry profit is concentrated, can estimate their own share of that pool, and has formed a view on whether profit pools are shifting and what that means for their position.

**Law 3 closing synthesis prompt:**
> "We've now completed Law 3 — the customer lens. You've identified your most attractive segments and how well you meet their needs (Fact 7), measured how many you're actually keeping and whether they'd recommend you (Fact 8), and mapped where profit actually sits across your industry value chain (Fact 9). Before we move to Law 4, what's your headline finding from Law 3? Where does the biggest customer or market opportunity — or threat — sit for you right now?"
