# Fact 7: Customer Segments Analysis & Segment Needs and Performance (SNAP)
**Law:** Law 3 — [To be confirmed]
**Position in Law:** Fact 1 of 3

**Structure note:** This fact comprises two complementary parts sharing a single diagnostic identity in the source material. Part 1 identifies which customer segments are most attractive. Part 2 (SNAP) assesses how well you meet each segment's needs relative to competitors and substitutes. Part 1 tells you *where* to compete; Part 2 tells you *whether you can win*.

---

## Purpose
Identify which customer segments are genuinely worth pursuing — by size, growth, and profitability — then determine whether you can actually win them by comparing your performance against what those segments value most, relative to competitors and substitutes.

---

## Part 1: Customer Segments Analysis (7.1)

### Goal
Identify the most attractive segments of a company's customer base (existing or potential) by comparing segments' size, growth, and profitability. This analysis helps show how you can increase your growth and position on the ROA/RMS chart (Fact 4). It also helps answer "which segments should we be targeting?" — though answering this fully requires Part 2's assessment of your ability, compared with competitors, to meet those segments' needs.

**On segmentation approach:** There are many ways to segment a population — demographics, behaviours, beliefs, needs, or occasion of use. What you're looking for is a segmentation that really defines *how a customer chooses one product over another*. Examples:
- A family may belong to three segments simultaneously through one household: the sports car is for the middle-aged husband, the minivan for the soccer mom, the economy sedan for the teenage daughter — segmentation by need/role, not by household demographics
- A food company found a segment of ice-cream-topping lovers willing to pay a premium — segmentation by occasion of use. The same product (chocolate syrup) is a commodity in one context (baking) and a premium item in another (ice cream topping)

### Diagnostic Question
> "Which are the biggest, fastest-growing, and most profitable customer segments?"

### Data Required
- Identification of customer segments (according to needs, behaviours, and key characteristics)
- Current size and value of each customer segment
- Forecasted growth rates for each customer segment
- Profitability of each customer segment

### Analytical Approach

**Four-step process (Figure A3.14)**

| Step | Description | Key Success Factor |
|------|-------------|---------------------|
| 1. Identify customer segments | Preliminary identification | Based on needs, behaviours, or key characteristics; refine using advanced techniques (e.g. conjoint analysis, cluster analysis) |
| 2. Identify each segment's spending and forecasted growth | Size each segment | Gather intelligence (own data, external reports) and use surveys to estimate total segment spend |
| 3. Estimate each segment's profitability | Determine margin by segment | Estimate operating margin by mining your own data and using external surveys |
| 4. Map all segments on one chart; draw implications | Visualise and prioritise | Consider strategic and operational implications |

**Reading the chart (Figure A3.13):** A 2×2 matrix — Y-axis is Segment Profitability (Low to High), X-axis is Segment Growth (Low to High). The top-right quadrant is explicitly labelled "Segments to Focus On." Bubble size represents dollars of spend. This immediately reveals which segments deserve focus, which are sizable but unattractive, and whether your current spend is concentrated where it should be.

## Part 2: Segment Needs and Performance — SNAP Analysis (7.2)

### Goal
The SNAP chart shows the importance of different purchasing criteria to your customers, and reveals how customers rate both you and your competitors against those criteria. Using the chart, you can see at a glance what different segments find important, their perception of your offering, and how that compares with their perception of competitors. You can identify the biggest gaps between a criterion's importance and your performance, spotting where you may be overinvesting or underinvesting in meeting specific needs.

This analysis should be performed for **every major customer segment**, since purchasing criteria and performance vary by segment. If possible, compare against a SNAP chart from two to three years ago — the comparison reveals how customer needs have been shifting and what that may signal about potential shifts in profit pools.

### Diagnostic Question
> "How well do you meet customer needs relative to competitors and substitutes?"

### Data Required
- Specific attributes that are important to customers about the product/service
- Market research on the relative importance of these attributes
- Performance for your company and competitors on these attributes
- From this: identify critical gaps (e.g. move service levels from 96% to 99%)

### Analytical Approach

**Five-step SNAP process (Figure A3.16)**

| Step | Description | Key Success Factor |
|------|-------------|---------------------|
| 1. Identify customer segments to survey | Decide who to ask | Segmentation becomes even more critical as markets mature; too broad a definition can lead to over- or underinvestment, or tapping into less rich profit pools |
| 2. Pretest the purchasing criteria | Validate the criteria list | Pretest through open-ended questions to a small group of customers ("What is important to you and why?") |
| 3. Design and conduct customer survey | Gather the data | Use extreme statements (e.g. "How important is lowest possible price?" rather than "price") to avoid getting uniform 4s and 5s on all criteria |
| 4. Analyse data; compare to SNAP chart from 2–3 years ago | Spot the shifts | Important to highlight areas of overinvestment as well as underperformance |
| 5. Draw implications | Decide what to change | Formulate change action plans in product development, manufacturing, marketing, and sales activities |

**Reading the chart (Figure A3.15):** Bars show importance to customer (1–5 scale) for each purchasing criterion (sample categories: Quality, Price, Customer Service, Innovation, Time to Market, Assortment). Two overlaid lines show relative performance — one for your Company, one for Competitors. The most actionable insight comes from criteria with high importance bars where your performance line sits notably below the competitor line.

---

## Interpretation Guide

**Healthy signal:**
- High-growth, high-profitability segments (top-right quadrant of Part 1) are ones where you already have share and strong relative performance on the criteria that matter most to that segment
- Your performance line meets or exceeds competitors on the highest-importance criteria

**Warning signal:**
- Spend is concentrated in low-growth/low-profitability segments while "segments to focus on" are underserved
- A criterion customers rate as highly important shows you significantly underperforming competitors — a clear vulnerability
- You significantly outperform on a criterion customers rate as low importance — likely overinvestment and wasted resources

**Ambiguous result — probe further:**
- If segment profitability is hard to estimate, ask: which proxy (gross margin contribution, customer lifetime value) is most defensible given available data?
- If performance gaps versus competitors are small but stakes are high, probe for non-obvious differentiators — brand, relationship depth, switching costs — that the SNAP framework doesn't directly capture

## Additional Tools

**Diagnostic tools for customer segmentation:** lifetime customer value analysis, conjoint analysis, cluster analysis, CHAID (chi-square automatic interaction detector), perceptual mapping, customer ethnographic research, "voice of the customer" qualitative surveys, customer migration analysis, and the revenue sieve.

**Implementation tools:** focus on customer acquisition and development of your customer value proposition — the latter via marketing mix effectiveness, including product management and innovation, pricing strategy (tactical, strategic, price customization), and sales-channel management (channel, salesforce, store/brand strategy).

## Agent Prompting Guidance

**Opening question (Part 1):**
> "We're moving into Law 3 now — this shifts the lens onto your customers directly. First: which customer segments are biggest, fastest-growing, and most profitable for you? Do you already segment your customer base in a meaningful way, or is that something worth working through together?"

**Follow-up probes if stuck (Part 1):**
- "How do you currently define your customer segments — by demographics, behaviour, need, or occasion of use?"
- "Which segment generates the most revenue today? Which one is growing fastest?"
- "Is there a segment you suspect is highly profitable but currently underserved by your focus?"

**Synthesising the finding (Part 1):**
> "So your most attractive segment looks like [segment] — strong on both growth and profitability — but your current focus seems concentrated on [segment]. Does that mismatch feel accurate?"

**Transition to Part 2:**
> "Now that we know which segments matter most, let's check whether you can actually win them. For your top segment, what do customers say matters most when choosing a provider — quality, price, service, something else? And how do you think you stack up against competitors on those specific things?"

**Follow-up probes if stuck (Part 2):**
- "If you surveyed your top customers today, what would they say is most important to them?"
- "On those top criteria, are you ahead of, level with, or behind your main competitors?"
- "Is there a criterion where you might be overinvesting relative to how much customers actually value it?"

**Synthesising the finding (Part 2):**
> "It sounds like [criterion] is the most important factor for your top segment, and you're currently [ahead of/behind] competitors there. The biggest gap appears to be in [criterion]. Does that match your read, and does it tell you where to focus first?"

**Signal to move on:**
User has identified their most attractive segment(s), articulated the purchasing criteria that matter most to those segments, and formed a view on where they are winning or losing against competitors on those specific criteria.
