# Fact 8: Customer Retention Analysis & Net Promoter Score (NPS)

**Structure note:** Like Fact 7, this fact comprises two complementary parts under a single diagnostic identity. Part 1 establishes the retention baseline and diagnoses why customers leave. Part 2 (NPS) gives a forward-looking, competitively benchmarked metric for the loyalty that drives future retention and growth.

---

## Purpose
Establish how many customers you are actually keeping — and why the rest leave — then layer on a forward-looking loyalty metric that predicts future retention and organic growth, benchmarked directly against competitors.

---

## Part 1: Customer Retention Analysis (8.1)

### Goal
Establish a baseline: what proportion of your customers in each of your segments are you retaining? From there, assess the impact of retaining (or not retaining) specific segments, and analyse the root causes of customer defection.

**Key insight on defectors:** Satisfaction is usually built on a broad foundation of customer touch points and product attributes. But when a customer stops using your product, they can usually tell you exactly why with just one or two reasons. It is much easier to determine why customers are leaving than why they are staying. If you can eliminate the reasons for defection, you will end up retaining more customers. The ideal is zero defections.

**Key insight on spend, not just count:** Analyse retention not only by number of customers but by level of spending. Are you losing your highest-spending (and most profitable) customers? Are customers whom you retain increasing their spend with you over time? This was a key insight for American Express — it was adding many customers each year, but the real growth came from increased spending of its best customers.

*For more detail, see Fred Reichheld's "The Loyalty Effect."*

### Diagnostic Question
> "What proportion of customers are you retaining?"

### Data Required
- Total number of customers at the beginning of Year 1 and Year 2
- New customers added during Year 1

### Analytical Approach

**Customer Retention Rate Formula (Figure A3.18):**
```
RR = (Customers in Year 2 − New Customers) / Customers in Year 1
```
This isolates the customers who were present at the start of Year 1 and are still present in Year 2 (by subtracting newly acquired customers from the Year 2 count), then divides by the starting base.

**Worked example (Figure A3.17):** If Year 1 had 30% of customers defect by year end, the retention rate is 70%. In Year 2, even with 20% of the base being new customers, the underlying retention rate remains 70% — the metric isolates true retention from growth via new acquisition.

**Four-step process (Figure A3.18)**

| Step | Description | Key Success Factor |
|------|-------------|---------------------|
| 1. Define "the customer" | Choose an actionable unit with available data | E.g. for a bank, "the customer" can be a single account, an individual, or a household |
| 2. Define "retention" | Set the threshold that counts as retained | E.g. for a supermarket: minimum $100 monthly spend for twelve consecutive months; for insurance: annual policy renewal |
| 3. Calculate retention rate | Apply the RR formula | RR = (Customers Year 2 − New Customers) / Customers Year 1 |
| 4. Perform root cause analysis and draw implications | Understand why people leave | Determine root causes of defection (interview/survey defectors); consider strategic and operational ways to increase retention in most attractive segments |

## Part 2: NPS Analysis (8.2)

### Goal
Net Promoter Score (NPS) gives a simple, powerful way to track your ability to retain customers, based on one question: "How likely is it that you would recommend our company (or product or service) to a friend or colleague?" Respondents answer on a zero-to-ten scale.

- **9–10 = Promoters** — likely to spend more with you, increase their spending over time, and spread positive word of mouth
- **7–8 = Passives**
- **0–6 = Detractors**

**Formula:**
```
NPS = % Promoters − % Detractors
```

There is a big difference between eliminating the poor performance that drives customers away, and developing attributes that truly delight customers. It is the latter that leads customers to promote your product to friends and neighbours — and doing this greatly enhances your potential for gaining market share.

NPS is most powerful when tracked over time by segment, and when compared with competitors' scores. Done right, it instils an "NPS discipline" within an organisation — a set of processes that keeps everyone focused on driving customer loyalty.

*For a detailed discussion, see Fred Reichheld's "The Ultimate Question."*

### Diagnostic Question
> "How does your NPS rating track against competitors?"

### Data Required
- Well-designed, simple Net Promoter Score survey with high customer response rates to track data for your company
- Periodic evaluation of your NPS relative to key competitors

### Analytical Approach

**Five-step process (Figure A3.20)**

| Step | Description | Key Success Factor |
|------|-------------|---------------------|
| 1. Identify primary objective | Decide the level of measurement | Company NPS? Business unit? Product? Other? Lower level is generally more actionable |
| 2. Identify key competitors | Set the benchmark | Use correct business definition; NPS is most powerful when compared to key competitors |
| 3. Conduct customer survey | Ask the ultimate question, plus why | In addition to the "ultimate question," include "why?" questions — separate for promoters versus detractors |
| 4. Analyse data | Compare and correlate | Compare NPS to competitors; if possible, correlate NPS to financial/product growth |
| 5. Draw implications | Turn the data into action | Use the NPS chart to draw strategic and tactical insights: competitive position, best-in-class benchmarks, required improvements among target customers, etc. |

**Reading the chart (Figure A3.19):** Bars for "Competitor" and "Company" show the proportion of Promoters versus Detractors. A "Point of Departure" marks current NPS; a "Point of Arrival" marks the targeted improved NPS. The visual makes the competitive gap and improvement trajectory immediately clear.

## Interpretation Guide

**Healthy signal:**
- Retention rate is stable or improving, particularly among highest-spending customers
- NPS is positive and trending upward, especially relative to key competitors
- Retained customers show increasing spend over time (the "American Express insight")

**Warning signal:**
- High overall customer count growth masks declining retention among your most valuable customers
- NPS is declining or significantly behind competitors despite stable retention numbers — a leading indicator that retention will deteriorate
- A small number of recurring, specific reasons keep showing up in defector interviews and remain unaddressed

**Ambiguous result — probe further:**
- If retention looks healthy but NPS is weak, ask: are customers staying out of inertia or switching cost rather than genuine satisfaction? This is a vulnerability, not a strength.
- If defector reasons are vague, ask: have you actually interviewed people who left, or are you inferring reasons internally?

## Additional Tools

**Diagnostic tools:** in-depth analyses of key drivers (through customer surveys), root-cause analytic trees that identify reasons for defection, and analysis of share of wallet and how it is trending over time.

**Implementation tools:** customer value-proposition development (see Fact 7); customer-relationship management (CRM); creation of an NPS culture; and specific initiatives such as early-engagement programs, "save the sale" programs, loyalty and rewards programs, service recovery plans, and "zero defections" programs.

## Agent Prompting Guidance

**Opening question:**
> "Let's look at how well you're actually keeping the customers you've identified as most valuable. Do you track a customer retention rate today — and do you know if you're losing your highest-spending customers, or mostly lower-value ones?"

**Follow-up probes if stuck (Part 1):**
- "How does your business define 'the customer' — an account, an individual, a household?"
- "What would count as 'retained' for your business — ongoing spend, contract renewal, repeat purchase?"
- "Have you ever interviewed customers who left? What reasons came up most often?"
- "Are the customers you retain spending more with you over time, or roughly the same?"

**Synthesising the finding (Part 1):**
> "So your retention rate looks to be around [X]%, and the main reasons customers leave seem to be [reasons]. [Notable insight about high-value customer retention]. Does that match what you're seeing?"

**Transition to Part 2:**
> "Now let's look forward, not just back — how likely are your customers to actually recommend you? Have you measured NPS, and if so, do you know how it compares to your key competitors?"

**Follow-up probes if stuck (Part 2):**
- "If you don't track NPS formally, what's your gut sense — are most customers enthusiastic promoters, lukewarm, or actively unhappy?"
- "Do you know how your competitors' NPS compares to yours?"
- "When customers do promote you, what specifically do they say delights them?"

**Synthesising the finding (Part 2):**
> "It sounds like your NPS is roughly [estimate/known value], compared to competitors at [estimate]. The gap seems driven by [reason]. Combined with what we found on retention, does this change how urgently you'd prioritise fixing [specific issue]?"

**Signal to move on:**
User has a working retention rate, has identified the dominant reasons for defection, and has a view (measured or estimated) of their NPS relative to competitors.
