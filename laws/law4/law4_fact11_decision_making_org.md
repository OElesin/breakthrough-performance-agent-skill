# Fact 11: Decision-Making and Organisational Complexity Analysis
**Law:** Law 4 — [To be confirmed]
**Position in Law:** Fact 2 of 3

**Structure note:** Another compound fact with two complementary diagnostics. Part 1 (RAPID Analysis) examines the complexity of how decisions are made. Part 2 (Spans-and-Layers Analysis) examines the complexity of the organisational structure itself. Together they reveal whether your organisation is built for speed and accountability, or is slowed and blurred by structural overhead.

---

## Purpose
Determine whether your decision-making processes and organisational structure are competitive — diagnosing where complexity is adding cost and slowing execution, and what it would take to reach a leaner, faster, more accountable organisation.

---

## Part 1: RAPID Analysis (11.1)

### Goal
Improve decision-making processes by codifying individuals' roles in key decisions. RAPID provides a degree of analytical rigour in an area that most organisations never examine at all.

**RAPID is a loose acronym for the five key roles in any decision:**
- **R — Recommend:** Gathers relevant facts, obtains input from the appropriate people, and makes a recommendation
- **A — Agree (Veto):** Must approve the recommendation; has veto power
- **P — Perform:** Responsible for carrying out the decision once made
- **I — Input:** Provides facts and judgement about the best course of action
- **D — Decide:** The single person with the ability to make the final decision

**Key rules:**
- There is only one D and one R per decision
- There can be multiple I and P roles
- Use A (veto) sparingly

**Why it matters:** Streamlining decision-making reduces costs. It increases organisational nimbleness by reducing labour time, meeting time, and time spent revisiting decisions or determining who should be making them. It encourages focused management behaviour, eliminates redundant analyses, and ensures accountability.

*For more detail, see "Who Has the D?" by Paul Rogers and Marcia Blenko, Harvard Business Review, January 2006.*

### Diagnostic Question
> "How complex is your decision making relative to competitors? What is the impact of this complexity?"

### Data Required
- List of top decisions (e.g. what new products to launch, what pricing to set, what service model to use, what positioning by segment)
- Opinions from different levels and departments on who holds various decision rights (RAPID)
- Evaluation of the impact on the organisation of current decision-making processes

### Analytical Approach

**Reading the RAPID chart (Figure A3.25):** A matrix with Decisions in rows and Roles/Functions in columns (e.g. Segment Managers, Product Managers, Global Marketing, Legal, Channels, Exec Team). Each cell shows who holds which RAPID role for that decision. Common examples:
- "What new products" — D sits with one function, R with another, I with several
- "What pricing" — may have D in two places (a warning sign of ambiguity)

**Four-step process (Figure A3.26)**

| Step | Description | Key Success Factor |
|------|-------------|---------------------|
| 1. Establish key processes and decisions | Identify what needs to be decided | First establish core business processes (e.g. develop products, manage brands); then determine key management decisions within each process (e.g. for managing brands → packaging, pricing, promotion) |
| 2. Design the organisation "superstructure" | Define the structural context | Develop a basic outline: How many levels? What process/functional heads? Organised regionally, globally, or by product? The RAPID method is applied to each decision within the context of this superstructure |
| 3. Define decision accountabilities | Assign RAPID roles | Put in place guiding principles about how decisions should be made (e.g. end-to-end process should be visible in all top decisions); only one D, one R; use A sparingly |
| 4. Design the organisation | Build the right structure around the decisions | Determine roles at various levels; define the scope of each job; identify the right person for each job (and tailor the job to the individual where possible) |

## Part 2: Spans-and-Layers Analysis (11.2)

### Goal
Spans-and-layers analysis examines overhead by measuring:
- **Spans:** The number of direct reports per manager
- **Layers:** The number of management levels between the CEO and frontline employees

Applying the analysis enables a company to: lower overhead costs; reduce complexity in the organisation; simplify decision making; and increase the level of accountability and innovation.

### Diagnostic Question
> "How complex is your organisation relative to competitors? What is the impact of this complexity?"

### Data Required
- Comprehensive organisational chart showing number of layers and departments/divisions
- Organisational chart for each level showing total number of direct reports

### Analytical Approach

**Two levels of analysis:**

**High-level view (Figure A3.27):** A layer-by-layer table showing Current Span vs Target Span of Control for each management level. Example: Layer 1 (CEO) — Current Span 12.0, Target 12; Layer 2 — Current 6.8, Target 10; Layer 9 — Current 5.0, Target 15; Average current span 8.3, Average target 13.3. This immediately shows where spans are narrower than they should be, implying too many managers or too many layers.

**Detailed view (Figure A3.28):** A circular organisational chart (spider diagram) showing every node in the organisation with span-of-control coding: filled circle = span >7, grey circle = span 5–7, open circle = span <5. Median span in the example: 5.7. This detailed view shows exactly how many people report to each individual manager throughout the organisation.

**Four-step process (Figure A3.29)**

| Step | Description | Key Success Factor |
|------|-------------|---------------------|
| 1. Map current spans and layers | Establish the baseline | Combine interviews with data collection to assess current spans and layers |
| 2. Identify target benchmarks | Set the comparison standard | Use a combination of external (competitor) and internal (other divisions) benchmarks |
| 3. Analyse spans and layers against benchmarks | Find the gaps | Identify root causes for short spans/deep layers; compare to benchmarks adjusting for job complexity differences |
| 4. Draw implications | Decide what to restructure | Determine improvement actions and impact on decision-making processes and culture; quantify savings |

## Interpretation Guide

**Healthy signal (RAPID):**
- Each key decision has a clear, unambiguous D — one person who owns the outcome
- R and D are distinct roles — the person recommending is not also the person deciding
- I roles are selective, not universal — not everyone is asked to weigh in on everything

**Warning signal (RAPID):**
- Multiple D roles on the same decision — a structural source of delay, conflict, or diffused accountability
- A (veto) used so broadly it effectively means the D is not really deciding
- Decisions loop back repeatedly — a sign that RAPID roles are unclear or ignored

**Healthy signal (Spans-and-Layers):**
- Spans are broad at lower levels (managers supervising 10–15 direct reports) — efficient use of management resource
- Layers between CEO and frontline are minimal — information travels fast and leadership is visible

**Warning signal (Spans-and-Layers):**
- Narrow spans throughout (managers with 3–4 direct reports) — excess management layers, slow decision velocity, high overhead cost
- Many layers between CEO and frontline — strategy is diluted and accountability diffuses as it travels down

## Agent Prompting Guidance

**Opening question:**
> "Now let's look at how your organisation makes decisions. For the most important decisions your business faces — what to build, what to price, what to cut — is it always clear who has the final call? Or do decisions tend to involve too many people and take longer than they should?"

**Follow-up probes if stuck (RAPID):**
- "Think of a decision that took longer than expected recently — why did it slow down? Too many approvals? Unclear ownership? Someone with veto power who wasn't engaged early enough?"
- "For a major decision like pricing or new product launch — can you name the single person who has the D?"
- "How many people are typically in the room when a key decision gets made?"

**Synthesising the finding (RAPID):**
> "It sounds like [decision type] has an unclear or contested D, which is likely causing [delay/conflict/rework]. The fix is probably clarifying who R and D are and giving A roles only to those who genuinely need veto power. Does that match the pattern you're seeing?"

**Transition to Spans-and-Layers:**
> "Now let's look at the structure itself. How many layers sit between you and the most junior people on your team? And roughly how many direct reports do your managers typically carry?"

**Follow-up probes if stuck (Spans-and-Layers):**
- "How many management levels are there between you and the frontline?"
- "Do your managers typically have 3–4 direct reports, or 10–12?"
- "Where does information seem to get slowed or filtered as it travels up and down the organisation?"

**Synthesising the finding (Spans-and-Layers):**
> "With [X] layers and an average span of [Y], your structure looks [lean/heavy] relative to what's typically competitive. The overhead cost implication is [significant/moderate], and the decision-speed implication is [high/low]. Does restructuring feel like a near-term priority or a longer-term one?"

**Signal to move on:**
User has a clear view of where decision ambiguity sits (which decisions lack a clear D), and a working read on whether spans are too narrow and layers too deep relative to competitive benchmarks.
